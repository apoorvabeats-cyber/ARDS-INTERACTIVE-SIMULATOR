STEP 1

Enter Calcium Level

If albumin is entered, calcium is corrected: Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin). Ionised calcium is more reliable in critical illness. 1 mmol/L = 4.0 mg/dL.

STEP 2 · HYPOCALCAEMIA

Urgency & Risk Assessment

Symptoms, QT interval and haemodynamics decide whether IV calcium is needed now, not the number alone.

STEP 3 · HYPOCALCAEMIA

Determine the Mechanism

PTH is the key test: in hypocalcaemia it should be high. Send PTH, 25-OH vitamin D and phosphate before large calcium doses where possible, and treat the symptomatic patient first.

📖 Hypocalcemia Protocols & References

          
        

        

          

            
1. IV Calcium Safety Limits

            
    
              
  • Bolus: 10% calcium gluconate 10–20 mL (1–2 g = 93–186 mg elemental) in 50–100 mL over 10–20 min, with ECG monitoring. Gluconate is safe peripherally; calcium chloride (272 mg elemental per g) goes via a central line only.
    • 
              
  • Infusion: 0.5–1.5 mg elemental Ca/kg/h (e.g. 100 mL of 10% gluconate in 900 mL NS or D5W ≈ 0.93 mg/mL), titrated to the level.
    • 
              
  • Do not mix with bicarbonate or phosphate (precipitates). Avoid ceftriaxone in the same line.
    • 
              
  • Digoxin: give slowly with monitoring; calcium potentiates toxicity.
    • 
              
  • Extravasation causes tissue necrosis — check the cannula.
    • 
            

          

          

            
2. Estimate Used Here

            

              
Corrected Ca = Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin g/dL)

              
Serum rise ≈ 0.12–0.25 mg/dL per mg/kg elemental Ca

              
10 mL 10% Ca gluconate = 93 mg elemental = 2.3 mmol

            

            
An IV bolus raises calcium for only 2–3 h, so follow it with an infusion and treat the cause. Approximations only — titrate to level.

          

          

            
3. Fix the Cause

            
Replace Mg²⁺ first (low Mg²⁺ causes PTH failure and resistance). Give calcitriol 0.25–0.5 mcg twice daily in hypoparathyroidism and CKD; cholecalciferol for deficiency. In hyperphosphataemia, treat the phosphate and avoid forcing calcium (Ca × P product >55 risks calcification).

          

          

            
4. Oral Therapy

            
Elemental calcium 1–3 g/day in divided doses with meals (carbonate = 40% elemental; use citrate with PPI or achlorhydria). Start oral therapy and vitamin D before stopping the infusion.

          

          

            
5. Monitoring

            
    
              
  • Ca²⁺ (ideally ionised) every 4–6 h during infusion, every 1–2 h if symptomatic.
    • 
              
  • Continuous ECG while symptomatic or QT is prolonged. Check Mg²⁺, phosphate and K⁺.
    • 
              
  • Aim for low-normal calcium; avoid overshoot.
    •

📖 Hypercalcemia Protocols & References

          
        

        

          

            
1. Severity (corrected calcium)

            
    
              
  • Mild 10.6–11.9 · Moderate 12.0–13.9 · Severe ≥14.0 mg/dL.
    • 
              
  • Severe symptoms (confusion, coma), ECG changes (short QT, bradycardia, block) or AKI make it an emergency at any level ≥12.
    • 
            

          

          

            
2. Treatment Order

            

              
Fluids: 0.9% saline 1–2 L bolus, then 100–250 mL/h aiming for urine output 100–150 mL/h. Be cautious in heart failure or CKD.

              
Calcitonin: 4 IU/kg SC/IM every 12 h; onset 4–6 h; effect fades in ~48 h.

              
Zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over ≥15 min (reduce for eGFR 30–59; avoid below 30) or pamidronate 60–90 mg over 2–4 h. Onset 2–4 days, lasts weeks.

              
Denosumab 120 mg SC — option for eGFR <30 or refractory disease; watch for hypocalcaemia.

              
Glucocorticoid (prednisolone 20–40 mg/day) only for vitamin D–mediated causes.

              
Dialysis with low-calcium dialysate for severe disease with renal failure or heart failure.

            

          

          

            
3. Loop Diuretics

            
Not routine. Use furosemide only after volume repletion or if overloaded; used early they worsen dehydration and hypercalcaemia.

          

          

            
4. Treat the Cause

            
Stop thiazides, lithium, calcium and vitamin D. Primary hyperparathyroidism: parathyroidectomy (cinacalcet if unfit). Malignancy: treat the tumour and repeat bisphosphonate / denosumab. Sarcoid or vitamin D toxicity: glucocorticoid.

          

          

            
5. Monitoring

            
    
              
  • Ca²⁺ (ideally ionised) at 6, 12, 24 h, then daily; check K⁺, Mg²⁺, phosphate and creatinine.
    • 
              
  • Fluid balance and signs of overload during saline. Continuous ECG if QT shortening or arrhythmia.
    • 
              
  • After denosumab or bisphosphonates watch for late hypocalcaemia and hypophosphataemia.
    •
